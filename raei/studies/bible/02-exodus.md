## 1. Setting, genre and structure

Exodus continues Genesis' ending: Jacob's family has settled in Egypt, but generations later the Israelites are oppressed. The book narrates deliverance, covenant formation and the construction of the tabernacle. Its literary forms include narrative, law, covenant instruction, genealogy and detailed sanctuary description.

A useful outline:

1. **Oppression and deliverance (1:1–15:21):** Israel's oppression, Moses' call, confrontation with Pharaoh, plagues, Passover, departure and sea deliverance.
2. **Journey and covenant (15:22–24:18):** wilderness provision, Sinai, the Ten Commandments and covenant ratification.
3. **Covenant rupture and renewal (25:1–34:35):** tabernacle instructions, golden calf, Moses' intercession and covenant renewal.
4. **Tabernacle construction and divine presence (35:1–40:38):** willing contributions, skilled work, completion and the glory filling the tabernacle.

The book's centre is not merely escape from Egypt. Deliverance leads to covenant life and worship in God's presence.

## 3. Major themes

### Deliverance and covenant purpose
Exodus presents God hearing Israel's oppression and acting in faithfulness to the ancestral promises (2:23–25; 3:6–10). The deliverance is directed toward service and covenant relationship, not simply release from discomfort.

### God's name and character
Exodus 3:13–15 and 34:5–7 are foundational passages for the book's presentation of God's identity and character. Read each in its narrative setting: the call of Moses and the renewal after Israel's idolatry.

### Passover and remembrance
Exodus 12 connects deliverance with a recurring communal remembrance. The text's original Israelite setting must be preserved when later Christian canonical connections are considered.

### Law and redeemed identity
The covenant instructions follow the deliverance narrative. Israel is not portrayed as earning the exodus by law-keeping; the commandments shape the life of the people brought out of slavery. The Decalogue (20:1–17) begins with the identity of the God who delivered Israel.

### Idolatry, intercession and covenant renewal
The golden calf episode (32–34) exposes how quickly the people violate covenant loyalty. Moses' intercession and the renewal narrative hold together divine holiness, judgment, mercy and the seriousness of covenant breach.

### Worship, craftsmanship and presence
The tabernacle chapters show that worship involves materials, skilled labour, communal contribution and careful obedience. The completion scene (40:34–38) brings the book's movement to a climax: the divine presence fills the sanctuary.

## 4. Canonical relationships

- **Genesis:** the promise to the ancestors provides the narrative background for the exodus.
- **Leviticus and Numbers:** the tabernacle, priesthood, holiness and wilderness journey continue directly.
- **Deuteronomy:** Moses later recalls and interprets the exodus and covenant for the next generation.
- **Psalms and Prophets:** the exodus becomes a central memory of God's deliverance and a basis for calling Israel to covenant faithfulness.
- **Luke 9:28–36:** the Greek text uses the term often translated as “departure” for Jesus' coming death at Jerusalem; a relationship to the exodus motif is a plausible canonical resonance, not a replacement for Luke's immediate narrative.
- **1 Corinthians 10:1–13:** Paul uses wilderness events as warnings for the church. His application should be read in its argument about idolatry, testing and perseverance.

## 5. Interpretive cautions

1. Do not reduce the exodus to a generic metaphor for personal success. The text narrates God's action for an oppressed people and their covenant vocation.
2. Do not detach the plagues from the conflict with Pharaoh, the oppression and the book's theological claims.
3. Hardening language concerning Pharaoh is complex. Read the full sequence and distinguish the different grammatical subjects and narrative statements; do not simplify it into a slogan.
4. Do not claim the text supplies every archaeological or chronological detail modern readers may ask for.
5. Do not treat Israel's laws as a context-free modern statute book. Identify covenant setting, addressee, literary function and later canonical interpretation.
6. Do not spiritualise the tabernacle details so aggressively that the actual narrative emphasis on worship, craft, obedience and presence disappears.
7. Distinguish the text's explicit claims from theological synthesis and from contested historical reconstructions.

## 6. Comprehension questions with model responses

**Q1. How does Exodus connect with Genesis?**  
It continues the story of Jacob's family in Egypt and develops the ancestral promises against the setting of oppression and deliverance.

**Q2. What is the purpose of Moses' call in Exodus 3?**  
God commissions Moses to confront oppression and lead Israel out, grounding the mission in God's awareness of suffering and faithfulness to the ancestors.

**Q3. Is the exodus only an escape from Egypt?**  
No. The people are delivered into covenant relationship and worship. The book moves from oppression to Sinai and the tabernacle.

**Q4. Why does the Decalogue begin with a reminder of deliverance?**  
The commandments are given to a people already delivered. Their covenant obligations are grounded in God's prior action.

**Q5. What does the golden calf episode reveal?**  
It reveals covenant infidelity and the seriousness of idolatry, while the following chapters portray intercession, judgment, mercy and renewal.

**Q6. Why is the tabernacle important to Exodus' structure?**  
The book ends with the sanctuary completed and God's glory filling it. Deliverance leads to God's presence among the covenant people.

**Q7. What is the danger of reading Exodus 20 without Exodus 1–19?**  
It can detach the commandments from the deliverance and covenant identity that frame them.

**Q8. How does Paul use wilderness events in 1 Corinthians 10?**  
He uses Israel's experiences as warnings against idolatry and presumptuousness. His application should be interpreted within his argument to the Corinthian church.
